In the number of large non-distributed supercomputers on the Top500 list, the United States dominates with 171 systems, far ahead of China and Germany (both 40). Canada has 19 systems, placing it in the lower half of the benchmark group alongside France (23), South Korea (15), the United Kingdom (9), and Singapore (5). As for average download speed, Singapore leads at 308.8 Mbps, followed by the United States (257.7 Mbps) and France (250.0 Mbps). Canada records 192.9 Mbps, ranking in the middle tier and slightly outperforming China (191.0 Mbps), but trailing the leading countries. Note that South Korea data was not available for this sub-indicator. In GPU access measured by the number of AI research papers citing high-end GPUs (A100, H100, or TPU) in 2024–2025, China (169) and the United States (158) far outpace all other countries. Canada recorded only 20 high-end GPU citations, slightly above France (19) but notably behind Germany (37), South Korea (33), and the United Kingdom (29). Lastly, in integrated circuit exports, China ($171.2B) and South Korea ($139.4B) lead significantly, followed by Singapore ($73.0B) and the United States ($48.2B). Canada’s IC exports are the lowest in this group at $0.7B, reflecting its minimal role in semiconductor manufacturing. To conclude, Canada’s infrastructure gap is most pronounced in computing capacity and semiconductor manufacturing. While connectivity is relatively adequate, the shortfall in high-performance computing systems and GPU access suggests that Canada should prioritize investments in sovereign compute infrastructure and semiconductor supply chain participation to improve its AI competitiveness. The United States, China, and South Korea are the key countries Canada can learn from in this domain.
